## Key Question 1 — How 7-Eleven / FamilyMart / CP All select brands, suppliers and SKUs

### Takeaway
Seven-Eleven Japan runs a two-layer system: HQ merchandising teams (joint-developed private label through ~30 product subcommittees and a 70-company vendor cooperative with 92% dedicated factories) decide *what can be listed*, and each franchisee decides *what and how much to order* item-by-item using a hypothesis→order→verify loop on POS data, with an OFC coaching ~7–8 stores. Roughly 100–200 new SKUs enter weekly and ~70% of the ~2,500–3,000-item range turns over each year; poor sellers are simply dropped. No evidence of slotting/listing fees in Japanese konbini; the leverage runs the other way (HQ pushes manufacturers on PL costs, policed by the Antimonopoly Act).

**What this means for an Indian store:** the engine is not software, it is a *rule*: every item gets a weekly hypothesis (who buys it, when, why), an order quantity, and a verify step on POS; anything that misses twice is cut and replaced. Consto POS should force an item-level "why did I order this" note and show sell-through by hour/day/weather.

**1c. Listing / slotting fees and supplier terms**

- I found **no source stating that Japanese konbini charge slotting/listing fees**; TOO International's merchandising guide explicitly notes "No listing fees mentioned" — [TOO International](https://www.toointernational.com/post/key-merchandising-practices-of-japanese-convenience-store-chains). (Japan)
- The leverage runs *toward* the chain: Japanese business press reports leading food manufacturers "forced to bear burdens" in konbini PL practices, and 7-Eleven renegotiated purchase costs with manufacturers when it cut prices — [Diamond Online](https://diamond.jp/articles/-/228778). (Japan, 2020)
- Guardrail: Japan's Antimonopoly Act amendment (June 2009, effective 1 Jan 2010) made "abuse of superior bargaining position" subject to a surcharge of 1% of the relevant transaction value — [Shokusan (Food Industry Center)](https://www.shokusan.or.jp/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/454pdf4.pdf). (Japan)
- Contrast — US grocery: "initial slotting fee for a new product is around $250–$1,000 per item per store" — [Repsly](https://www.repsly.com/blog/consumer-goods/slotting-fee-negotiation-strategy); US c-store "listing fee" bundles database set-up, introductory allowance, free cases, launch participation — [Neomarketways](https://neomarketways.com/convenience-store-listing-costs-fees-margins-and-trade-promotions/). (USA)
- 7-Eleven US launched its own 7-Select private-label OTC drug line in Nov 2014 — [CSP Daily News](https://www.cspdailynews.com/general-merchandise/7-eleven-rolls-out-private-label-otc-drugs). (USA, 2014)

**What this means for an Indian store:** at one store you have no fee leverage; the only "fee" worth asking distributors for is free fill / sale-or-return on a first order of a new SKU. Model slotting as 0.

### Inferences
- The decision split that makes Tanpin Kanri work is: HQ owns the *catalogue* (~3,000 live SKUs, 100–200 swapped weekly), the store owns the *order quantity per SKU*. For Consto, the AI agent should play the OFC role (hypothesis prompts, weather/event cues), while the owner keeps the final order.
- A practical delisting rule consistent with the 70%/year churn: ~50–60 SKUs dropped per week per 3,000 = ~2% of range weekly. For a 1,200-SKU Indian store that is ~20–25 SKUs reviewed/dropped per week.
- "One to three facings" and "X units/week or delist" thresholds are widely repeated in retail folklore but I found **no primary 7-Eleven document** stating a numeric delisting threshold (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- Could not open the HBS case (scanned PDF) or Seven & i's *Store Business Partner* page (403), so there is no primary quote on the exact weekly OFC meeting cadence (Tuesday all-hands) or on the numeric "sell X/week or delist" rule.
- No primary CP All document on SME onboarding criteria (minimum volumes, DC delivery capability, listing fees); the CP All supplier page returned 403.
- No source on FamilyMart's or Lawson's PL development process at the same granularity as Seven & i's 2017 outline.
- No source on how many brands per sub-category konbini carry.

## Key Question 2 — 24-hour operating model: where it is used, what it earns, and Indian rules

### Takeaway
24h is still the default in Japan (≈90% of 7-Eleven/FamilyMart/Lawson stores as of Apr 2025, trending toward <80% within five years) and in Thailand/Korea/Taiwan; the 2019 Higashi-Osaka dispute forced all three Japanese chains to allow shortened hours, and 7-Eleven Japan now pays franchisees a royalty discount for staying 24h. The 23:00–07:00 window yields only ~16% of daily sales (JFA), but chains argue that dropping it cuts *total* sales ~30% because daytime traffic falls too. In India, Telangana (Apr 2023) and Delhi (Aug 2025) now allow 24x7 by exemption with fees/conditions; 7-Eleven India opened "24h" but ran 7am–11pm.

### Inferences
- The Japanese data say the night is a *traffic-protection* investment, not a profit centre: ~16% of sales but ~30% total-sales loss if dropped. For a Beeramguda store with ₹60–80k/day sales, the night window is ₹9–12k/day; one night worker at ₹600–700/shift is cheap against that.
- Night-time product mix (Korea data) is snacks, ice cream, instant noodles, fizzy drinks, bakery — plus, in India, milk/eggs at dawn and medicines (see Q3). Alcohol/cigarettes are hour-restricted by excise/COTPA rules, so do not plan the night around them.
- Delhi's Aug 2025 blanket exemption and Telangana's 2023 GO suggest a national drift toward permission-by-undertaking; expect further states to follow.

### Gaps
- No current (2024–26) count of shortened-hours stores per chain from primary sources; Yano's "≈90%" is the best available (Apr 2025).

- No data on 7-Eleven India's current hours or store count in 2025–26, nor on Twenty Four Seven's actual night footfall.
- No Telangana/Hyderabad 2024–26 order found that changes the 2023 GO (searches returned only the 2023 order); treat the ₹10,000/year regime as current but confirm with the Labour Dept.

## Key Question 3 — Pharmacies / medicines inside or next to convenience stores

### Takeaway
Japan's "registered seller" model (2009) barely spread (0.7% of ~57,000 konbini sell OTC as of Feb 2023; Lawson ~320 stores, 7-Eleven ~33), so the May 2025 law lets stores hand over OTC without a pharmacist from ~spring 2027 after online consultation. Korea allows a closed list of 13 "safety household medicines" in c-stores since Nov 2012 (11 actually sold; expansion to up to 20 under discussion in 2026). Taiwan's Class B OTC needs no licence or pharmacist. Thailand's CP All runs 460+ pharmacist-staffed eXta Plus pharmacies in/next to 7-Eleven, while 7-Eleven itself sells "household remedies". India already lets a non-licensed shop sell a Schedule K list (ORS, bandages, repellents, petroleum jelly, nicotine gum, zinc tablets anywhere; paracetamol/antacids/balms only in villages ≤1,000 people), and a real pharmacy counter needs its own retail licence + pharmacist — Reliance runs ~1,000 Netmeds shop-in-shops this way.

**What this means for an Indian store (Beeramguda):** Legal today without any drug licence: ORS, first-aid dressings/bandages, repellents, petroleum jelly, nicotine gum, zinc tablets, insecticides/disinfectants, plus non-drug items (cosmetics-classified Vicks-type rubs are *not* covered by this exemption unless they are registered as cosmetics — check the specific SKU's licence type). A real pharmacy = separate premises/partition, Form 20/21 licence, resident pharmacist; the cheapest route is a franchised shop-in-shop (Netmeds/Apollo/MedPlus) or a pick-up point.

### Inferences
- Three workable Indian models, ranked by cost: (1) **Legal OTC shelf** (Schedule K items 12, 25–28, 33, 37) — zero licence, small basket but 24h; (2) **Online-pharmacy pick-up/locker** (Tata 1mg/Netmeds/Apollo 24|7) — no licence at the store if stock is dispensed by the licensed partner and only handed over sealed (confirm with Telangana DCA); (3) **Adjacent franchised pharmacy** with its own licence and pharmacist (eXta Plus model) — ~₹8–15 lakh class investment and pharmacist salary, but captures the night-medicine demand that drives Korea's ~80% antipyretic/analgesic share.
- The May 2022 draft (16 OTC drugs) would legalise paracetamol-in-grocery nationally; its status should be re-checked quarterly, as the June 2026 Schedule K amendment shows the Ministry is actively editing this schedule.
