# Investor note on the Consto research report

**Who is writing:** an angel who has put money into Indian retail and food, and watched 7-Eleven India (₹90 cr loss on ₹92 cr revenue, closed Sep 2026), Twenty Four Seven (owner exited 2024) and a handful of cloud kitchens die. I read `Products.md` in full and notes 04, 06, 07, 08 and 09, plus the September session archive.

**Date:** 6 October 2026. **Store assumed:** 700 sq ft, Beeramguda-type suburb, no cooking, vendor-supplied hot case, chiller, shelf, premix chai, 4–6 seats, services counter, 6am–11pm, WhatsApp Agent. Everything below is an assumption stated as one; Sateesh said he will decide by assumption rather than pilot, so I have written the assumptions down so that they can be argued with and later replaced by the first 90 days of POS data.

**The one-paragraph verdict.** The research is good and honest about its gaps. The business it describes is a SuperK-shaped box with a hot case and a WhatsApp number bolted on. On the report's own inputs, Store 1 is a break-even shop in the lean case (about ₹21,000 a month before the founder is paid, before depreciation and interest; payback on ₹18 lakh of roughly seven years) and a modest earner in the standard case (about ₹63,000 a month after a ₹25,000 founder salary; payback on ₹28 lakh in about 44 months). Neither number funds a second store on its own. The investable version of this company is not "a store"; it is "a repeatable 700 sq ft unit that pays back in 24 months", and that needs ₹36,000–48,000 a day of sales, not ₹27,000–40,000. The decisions that matter are the ones that move daily sales and gross margin per day, not the ones about seats, organic shelves and kitchens.

### 1.3 How to read it

1. **The store is a packaged-goods store with a food counter, not a food store.** Fresh (hot, chai, bakery) is 22–26% of sales but 40–37% of gross margin. That is the right shape, and it is why every margin point on hot food matters more than any other decision in the report.
2. **Lean is a job, not a business.** ₹21,000 a month with the founder working a daily shift and drawing nothing is below what a SuperK franchisee takes home (₹50,000 on ₹10 lakh GMV). The lean store only becomes an investment if sales reach ₹10.8 lakh with the lean cost base.
3. **Standard is a business with a slow payback.** 44 months is outside what I fund for a single store; I fund 18–30 months. The gap is ₹54,000 a month of profit, which is ₹2.2 lakh of extra sales at 24.6% contribution, or 3–4 margin points across the whole store, or ₹35,000 of cost out.
4. **The cheapest levers, in order:** (a) hot-food gross margin from 30% to 40% by a real wholesale contract (worth ₹10–13k a month); (b) staff from 5 to 4 with the founder on the floor (₹15k); (c) rent at ₹36 not ₹50 (₹10k); (d) own brand from 4 to 10 SKUs once the first 4 turn (₹16k); (e) chai from 80 to 150 cups (₹14k). Those five together are the difference between lean and a 30-month payback.
5. **What the capex number hides:** ₹18–28 lakh excludes three months of ramp-up losses (₹1.5–3 lakh), a cash float for AEPS cash-out (₹50,000–1 lakh), and the founder's own living cost for a year. The real cheque is ₹22–25 lakh lean, ₹32–35 lakh standard.

## Part 2. Where I disagree with the report (12 points)

**1. On how big the store is.**
> "FSSAI licence type [Basic Registration / State Licence from day one]. My pick: State Licence from day one; a ₹30 lakh-a-month store crosses ₹1.5 cr in months." (Decisions, #60); also §8.6 "A store doing ₹30–40 lakh a month".

A 700 sq ft suburban store does not do ₹30–40 lakh a month. That is ₹1–1.3 lakh a day, three to four times SuperK's ₹10 lakh at the same size, and the report's own note 09 models ₹9 lakh. Ratnadeep's 3,000 sq ft supermarkets with 30,000 SKUs and two DCs average under ₹1 crore a month. Fix the number everywhere; a lender who sees ₹30 lakh next to ₹8 lakh stops reading. Take the State Licence anyway (₹2,000 a year) because ₹8 lakh a month is ₹96 lakh a year and the 2026 threshold is secondary-sourced.

**2. On hot-food margin when the vendor cooks.**
> "Highest-margin shelf (FamilyMart counter 50.7%); Consto version is idli, pulihora, puffs, samosa, boiled/masala eggs" (§7 table).

FamilyMart fries Famichiki in the store from frozen stock it buys at factory cost. Consto buys a finished plate from a tiffin kitchen that has already taken its own margin. The report's note 07 says so itself: 35–45% "if wholesale rates are negotiated", 0–10% "if the vendor charges its own retail-bulk price", and the ₹6–8 idli / ₹7–10 samosa B2B range is "an unverified assumption". Then subtract 5% GST with no input credit, a 4-hour discard clock, and the 8pm markdown. I model 30% lean, 33% standard, and I would not accept a plan that shows more than 35% until a signed price list exists. The Japanese number is a trap: it describes a chain that controls the factory.

**3. On chai as the hero.**
> "make A (machine chai + Osmania biscuit) the hero for Store 1 ... earns the highest margin in the store and brings people in from 6am to 11pm" (§12.1).

The margin per cup is real; the rupees are not. 80 cups at ₹12 is ₹960 a day, ₹29,000 a month, ₹16,000 of gross margin. That is a traffic device, and a good one, but it is 10% of lean gross profit, not a hero. Second, a Beeramguda suburb already has ₹10 tea stalls on every corner; premix chai from a machine is not better than a tea stall's, it is only cleaner and faster. The Seven Café and CITY CAFE comparisons are coffee in countries with no street coffee culture. Keep the machine (rent it, ₹2,000 a month); drop the word hero; the hero in this format is the ₹30 idli plate that is hot at 7:10am when the tiffin centre has a queue, and the basket it pulls.

**4. On waste.**
> "5% ceiling for 90 days, then 3%; zero waste means the case was empty" (§12.4).

Japan is at 2–3% after 40 years, 87% of items with more than 24 hours of life, and AI ordering in every store. A new store with two new vendors, no sales history and a 4-hour fried clock will run 8–12% on hot food in months one and two. Budget 8% of fresh sales for 90 days, 5% for the next 90, and 3% as the year-two target. More important than the number: the vendor must share it. A firm-sale hot-case contract with 0% returns at Store 1 is the vendor's risk-free trade; insist on 10% return allowance for 60 days (the report's own default #4 in note 07) and a joint review of the daily order so the vendor's over-supply is their cost, not yours.

**5. On seating and the rest corner.**
> "Seating [6–10 seats facing the hot case / none in Store 1]. My pick: 6–10 facing the chai machine and hot case" (Decisions, #59); and §7: "Consto's 'come rest even if you don't buy'; Indonesia's lesson: seats must not replace buying".

Note 09 did the arithmetic and then ignored it: 60 sq ft of seats displaces 2.7 bays, about ₹27,000–30,000 of gross margin a month, and six seats at five turns a day and a ₹60 ticket earn ₹24,000 of margin at best, so the seats are break-even on space if they are full all day. In a suburb they will not be full all day; they will be full at 7:30am and 6pm and host two uncles and a phone charger the rest of the time. 7-Eleven Indonesia died on exactly this. "Come rest even if you don't buy" is a brand line that costs ₹30,000 a month. My position: four stools at a window bar (≈30 sq ft), no table, no customer washroom in Store 1 (cleaning, water, loitering), no "rest" promise on the signage. Add seats in Store 2 if Store 1's POS shows the 7–9am and 5–8pm dwell baskets justify it. This is a reversible decision; a washroom is not.

**6. On services.**
> "Services before SKUs. Every Asian winner and every Indian loser is explained by this. AEPS, BBPS, recharge, parcel holding and pre-orders should be live on day one, not Phase 2" (§12.7).

Half right. AEPS and BBPS through an aggregator cost nothing to switch on and give the Agent a phone number, so yes, day one. But the Asian winners' services (OXXO's 6,331 payment transactions a store, Taiwan's 100 million bills a year) are national rails with scale; in India the per-transaction commission is ₹2–10, the aggregator tables are not even public (note 06 gap), AEPS needs a cash float of ₹50,000–1 lakh sitting in the drawer, and every withdrawal is three minutes of a cashier's time at the one counter you have. ₹15,000–35,000 a month net is the honest range, which is what I have modelled. Parcel holding and print-from-WhatsApp are fine. Bus tickets and MeeSeva are a second staff member's job; skip them in Store 1. The earlier thread's "40% of revenue at 60% margin" is dead; make sure no deck resurrects it.

**7. On organic and healthy.**
> Note 08: "Give organic/natural/healthy ~6–8% of shelf (≈ one 4-ft gondola + a 1 m sugar-free/senior bay + an eggs/ghee cold spot) targeting ~5–8% of sales at a blended ~20% margin vs the store's ~12% average."

The report's own arithmetic says a 4 ft bay is worth ₹10,000–11,000 of gross margin a month. A gondola of 40–55 organic/natural SKUs at the lean store's ₹25,000 of sales and 18% margin earns ₹4,500; even at the standard store's ₹50,000 it earns ₹10,000, a wash. It also ties up ₹40,000–60,000 of slow stock (honey, A2 ghee, cold-pressed oil turn four to six times a year), and 8% of surveyed Indians are committed organic buyers, with price the top barrier in Tier-2. So: no organic department, no "organic" signage, no certified-organic staples. Carry 15–20 trust items on the shelves where they belong (jaggery next to sugar, ragi snacks next to biscuits, one jaggery/ghee/country-egg "named farm" facing, one sugar-free bay of 1 metre for the grandparents), and the weekly pre-ordered farm basket as a service with zero shelf risk. Decision: carry the items, not the category. Fully reversible either way, so it is not worth the founder's time to debate further.

**8. On the Agent and the ₹300 membership.**
> §7 table: "Loyalty / app ... Keep as the Consto Agent + a ₹300/6-month membership ... SuperK's ₹300 membership gets 35–40% uptake in Tier-3 AP/Telangana."

SuperK's membership works on a ₹3,000-a-month dry-grocery basket, where 10% cashback is worth ₹300 a month to the household. Consto's basket is a ₹107–120 top-up; ₹300 is three visits' worth of spend and the cashback on it is ₹10 a visit. The uptake will be a fraction of SuperK's. More important: the Agent is a cost line with an unproven revenue line. Its costs are small (template messages, API, consent management, the founder's time writing specs) and I have put them in tech and marketing. Its claimed value ("members spend 1.6×" from OPENPOINT with 19 million members) is not transferable to a store with 300 customers a day. Model the Agent as ₹10,000 a month of cost and zero revenue until the 90-day data shows a repeat-rate lift among opted-in customers against a control group. The earlier thread's human "agent" headcount with 10% commission is rightly gone; keep it gone.

**9. On the kitchen trigger.**
> §3.5: "so is a central kitchen only at Store 3+"; note 07: "the own kitchen only overtakes at roughly 300–600 plates/day of combined demand (3–4 stores or stores plus outside B2B sales)".

The direction is right, the number is low, and the fixed-cost table in note 07 is a best case: two cooks at ₹15,000 (household-cook wages, not commercial), one shift covering both a 5am breakfast batch and a 2pm snack batch, no supervisor, no vehicle, no attrition, no compliance. Part 3 reworks it: break-even is 600–750 plates a day, which is 5–6 stores at the plates per store I model, or 3 stores plus a 200-plate B2B route. And the capital at risk is ₹15–17 lakh, not ₹6–7 lakh, once deposits, working capital and six months of under-volume losses are counted. "Store 3+" should read "Store 4–5 signed, or Store 3 plus B2B contracts in hand".

**10. On density and the timing of Store 2.**
> §3.5: "Density or nothing ... Rulebook L3 (Stores 2–5 within 3–5 km) is the correct answer."

Density is the correct end state and the wrong first move. Store 1 in the lean case generates ₹21,000 a month; it cannot fund Store 2, and Store 2 opened on borrowed money before Store 1 has proved ₹10 lakh a month for six consecutive months is how 7-Eleven India got to 55 loss-making stores. The report's own warning in §3.5 point 3 should be paired with a gate: Store 2 only after Store 1 shows two consecutive quarters of ≥₹10 lakh sales, ≥22% gross profit after waste, and operating profit after a ₹25,000 founder salary. Part 4 sets the gate.

**11. On the capex number and what it leaves out.**
> Note 09: "A Consto 700 sq ft store with seating and a hot counter lands at roughly ₹16–19 lakh lean to ₹24–27 lakh standard, all-in".

"All-in" includes two months of working capital. It excludes: three to four months of ramp-up losses (the store will not open at ₹26,000 a day; it opens at ₹12,000–15,000 and climbs), the AEPS cash float, a 10-month deposit at the standard rent (₹3.15 lakh locked and at the landlord's mercy), the founder's living cost for a year, and the ₹1–1.5 lakh of own-brand inventory and label design if the 10-SKU list is launched on day one. The honest cheque is ₹22–25 lakh lean and ₹32–35 lakh standard. The gap between "the plan" and "the cheque" is where single-store founders run out of money in month five.

**12. On khata and free delivery, the two kirana habits the report adds without pricing them.**
> §11.3: "Add a digital khata in the Agent, capped per customer, WhatsApp reminders" and "Add 2 km batched WhatsApp delivery, not 10-minute".

Both are right as features and unpriced as costs. Khata: 33% of kirana customers use credit; if a third of Consto's ₹8 lakh goes on khata that is ₹2.7 lakh of receivables, 2–3% of which will not come back, and the kirana recovers it through a relationship Consto's staff do not have. Cap the whole book at ₹50,000, cap each customer at ₹1,000, open khata only for members with 10 paid visits, and auto-freeze at 14 days. Delivery: a rider is ₹12–15k a month; at 20 drops a day and a ₹120 basket that is ₹72,000 of sales and ₹15,000 of gross margin, so the rider eats the whole margin. Free delivery only above ₹300, ₹15 below it, two slots a day, and the water-can subscription on the same bike so the route has a base load. The report also hasn't priced the 20 L water line: it is ₹5–10 a can of margin and only pays if the bike is going anyway.

### 3.3 Break-even

- Plates a month to cover ₹1.85 lakh at ₹8 saving per plate: **23,100, i.e. about 770 a day**. At ₹10 saving (a very good vendor-vs-own spread): 615 a day. At ₹6 (a vendor who is already cheap): 1,030 a day.
- Plates per Consto store: the lean P&L's ₹1 lakh of hot food is about **95 plates a day**; the standard's ₹1.8 lakh is about **170**. Neither is proven; both are assumptions.
- Stores needed with no outside sales: **770 ÷ 95 ≈ 8 lean stores; 770 ÷ 170 ≈ 4.5 standard stores**. Call it **five to six stores** at a realistic mix.
- With a B2B route (the batter micro-vendor model: 15–20 kiranas, tea stalls and offices taking 10–15 plates each, 200 plates a day): **three standard stores plus the route**, or four lean ones.
- A shared cloud-kitchen slot at ₹40,000 a month instead of own premises and equipment removes ₹36,500 of rent and depreciation and adds ₹40,000 of slot rent, so it barely moves the break-even; its real value is that it is reversible (one month's notice versus a lease and ₹5 lakh of equipment that resells at 30–40%).

| **Total exposure** | **₹15–17 lakh** |
| Of which unrecoverable if it fails (civil work, losses, 60% of equipment value) | ≈ ₹9–11 lakh |

That is the capex of a second store, spent to earn ₹8 a plate.

### 3.5 Kill criteria, if a kitchen is ever built

Stop and revert to vendors (or sell the output B2B and stop supplying the stores) if any one holds for two consecutive months after month 3:

1. Food cost at the kitchen above 35% of the retail value of what it ships (note 07's threshold, and the cloud-kitchen industry's "nearly impossible above 35%").
2. Plates below 450 a day across stores and B2B by month 6, or below 600 by month 9.
3. Store-level hot-food gross margin less than 10 points better than the vendor contract it replaced (the kitchen is supposed to lift 30% to 45–50%; if it delivers 38%, it has failed).
4. Two cooks lost in any six-month window (Zepto Café's stated failure mode).
5. Any FSSAI improvement notice, or any hot-case stale-food complaint traced to the kitchen (7-Eleven India's reviews were "expired or stale food"; one such review on Google in a suburb is the end of the hot case).

### 3.6 The answer

**Vendors at Store 1 and Store 2, with a contract that behaves like a kitchen.** Specifically: two tiffin kitchens within 8 km (primary 80%, backup 20%), Consto's written spec (weight, chutney and sambar volumes, pack, label with hour-level use-by, delivery at ≥60°C by 6:30am and 3:30pm), a per-piece wholesale price fixed for 12 months with the 5% escalation the tender norm allows, 10% return allowance for 60 days then firm, daily UPI settlement, and a ₹30,000–50,000 equipment loan to the primary vendor (steamer, hot boxes) against a 12-month first-call clause. That is the Japanese dedicated-maker model in miniature and it captures five to eight of the kitchen's twenty margin points for zero capex. Build a kitchen only when three conditions hold together: Store 3 is signed and Stores 1–2 have run six months at ≥₹10 lakh; combined hot-food demand including a signed B2B route is ≥600 plates a day; and ₹15 lakh is in the bank that no store needs. Expect that to be month 18–24 at the earliest, and start with a shared slot for six months before buying anything. For idli and vada specifically, the batter is the only part worth owning early: a co-packed Consto batter (note 07 default #25) sold in the chiller and supplied to the vendor is the cheap step towards recipe control.
