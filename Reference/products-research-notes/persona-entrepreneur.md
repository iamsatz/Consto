# Persona review: the entrepreneur who has opened stores

**Who is writing.** Someone who has opened and run small food-and-grocery shops in Indian suburbs and district towns: dealt with landlords who change their mind after the deposit, bakery vans that stop coming the week a bigger shop opens, a cook who quit on Diwali morning, a Food Safety Officer who wanted the FSSAI certificate framed, and customers who ask for udhaar on day three. This is a review of `Products.md` (6 Oct 2026) and research notes 06, 07, 08 and 09, through one lens only: what actually happens on the ground in the first 90 days, and why stores die.

**Date:** 6 October 2026. **Store assumed:** 700 sq ft, ground floor, main road in Beeramguda (Ameenpur Municipality, Sangareddy district; not GHMC — confirm), rent ₹30,000, Sateesh in the store every morning for the first 90 days. **Revenue assumed:** ₹8–12 lakh a month in year one (SuperK's ₹10 lakh is the right benchmark; the report's "₹30–40 lakh a month" is a year-three number for a very good store). Every "my pick" below is an assumption, stated so Sateesh can overrule it. No "test it" answers; where I would normally test, I say what I would assume and why.

The report is good research. It is also written by someone who has not stood behind a counter at 6:10am waiting for a van. The disagreements below are about that gap.

## Part 1. Where I disagree with the report

### 1.1 The hero: premix machine chai will not be the hero in a Telugu suburb

> "**Recommendation:** make **A (machine chai + Osmania biscuit)** the hero for Store 1 ... runs on the cheapest machine, undercuts the ₹25 Irani cup, earns the highest margin in the store and brings people in from 6am to 11pm." (§12.1)

**What I'd actually do.** The hero of Store 1 is the morning tiffin plate: 3 idli + sambar + chutney at ₹30, with a boiled egg at ₹10 next to it, sold from 6:15 to 10:30. Chai is the companion, not the hero. And I would not sell premix. In Beeramguda nobody pays ₹25 for Irani chai; the comparison is the tea bandi at the corner selling real milk chai at ₹10–12. Premix "machine chai" is what people drink in offices because they have no choice; at home and on the street they call it "powder tea" and walk past it. The report's own research (§12.1 table) shows chai is only "partly" unique because tea stalls exist. They exist on every corner.

My pick, in order: (a) bend the no-cooking rule for one electric appliance set — induction plate for decoction plus an electric tea urn with fresh Vijaya milk — and sell real chai at ₹10, ginger ₹12. It is no gas, no cylinder, no hygiene risk beyond a kettle. Assumption: "no gas" was the real rule and "no cooking" was shorthand for "no kitchen". (b) If Sateesh will not bend, rent the premix machine at ₹800 a month as a convenience for the seating corner and stop calling it the hero. The toastie as "signature" is fine for 4–8pm and for kids; nobody in a suburb is going to make a morning habit of a ₹40 toastie.

**Why.** A hero has to be something the street already buys by habit, where you win on cleanliness, speed and consistency, not on novelty. Idli at 6:30 is that. Chai is that only if it tastes like chai.

### 1.2 The kitchen question: Store 1 and Store 3+

> "Default to vendors for everything in year one ... revisit the kitchen decision only on a demand trigger (≥ 300–400 plates/day across stores and B2B)." (note 07, KQ9)
> "a central kitchen only at Store 3+" (§3.5)

Sateesh asked the question straight, so here is the straight answer.

**Store 1: vendor, with one exception.** Do not build a kitchen, do not rent a cloud-kitchen slot, do not hire a cook. The note's arithmetic (425 plates a day to break even) is right in direction and optimistic in detail, because it does not count the founder's time or the morning the cook does not come. A 700 sq ft store will sell 80–150 hot plates a day in month one; a kitchen at that volume is a second business that eats the first.

The exception is idli. Idli steaming is not cooking in anybody's eyes, including the FSSAI officer's; it is the same class as the sandwich toaster. A ₹25,000 electric 54-idli steamer, vendor batter (local at ₹60/kg or iD as the fallback) and sambar/chutney in sealed cups from the vendor gives you: fresh idli every 90 minutes instead of a 6am delivery of idli that is cold by 9; no dependency on the van for the one item that is the hero; and a plate that costs ₹12–14 in materials instead of ₹20 bought-in. Assumption: Sateesh accepts "electric steam from bought batter" as heating, not cooking. If he does not, buy idli from the vendor and accept that the 9:30 customer gets a 6am idli.

**Store 3+: still not your own central kitchen. A "mother vendor" instead.** When there are three stores within 8 km, pick the best of the vendors you have used for two years, give that vendor a ₹2–3 lakh interest-free advance against equipment, your written recipes, your containers and your labels, and make Consto 60–70% of their business. You own the recipe, the brand and the demand; they own the cook problem, the LPG cylinder, the FSSAI manufacturing licence and the 4am alarm. This is what Seven-Eleven Japan did with the Delica makers, in miniature. Build your own kitchen only when you have five or more stores, a kitchen manager you would trust with ₹3 lakh of cash and a 5am shift, and the mother vendor has let you down twice. Assumption: a dependent vendor is cheaper and safer than an employee-run kitchen until at least five stores; the cloud-kitchen closures in note 07 (Zepto Café, Bistro, Snacc) are the evidence that even funded people cannot keep cooks.

### 1.3 The two-vendor idea: 80/20 across two hot-food vendors gets you the worst of both

> "two vendors so one late truck does not empty the case" (§8.7)
> "two (primary 80%, backup 20%) for hot tiffin" (note 07, KQ9 Q3)

**What I'd actually do.** One hot-tiffin vendor at 100%, and a backup that is a different kind of supply, not a second tiffin vendor. A vendor giving you 20% of 100 plates is sending you 20 idlis a day; you are nobody to him, he gives you what is left after his carts are loaded, and he drops you the first time the cart boy asks for more. Meanwhile your main vendor knows you are "trying someone else" and stops caring. In a small town both vendors are cousins anyway.

The backup is: batter in the chiller and the electric steamer (idli in 25 minutes), 40 eggs and an electric egg boiler (always), 20 frozen samosas and parottas in the freezer (microwave or griller), and the bakery's puffs. That covers a missed van without a second relationship to manage. Note 07's own KQ2 inference says almost this ("keep a chilled day-2 fallback rather than a second hot vendor"); the main report lost it in the summary.

### 1.4 Organic and natural: cut the 45 SKUs to 15, and call it "Intiki manchi", never organic

> "Give organic/natural/healthy ~6–8% of shelf (≈ one 4-ft gondola + a 1 m sugar-free/senior bay + an eggs/ghee cold spot) targeting ~5–8% of sales ... ~45 SKUs" (note 08, KQ8–KQ9)

Sateesh's second question was whether people in such places expect organic. **They do not.** Nobody in Beeramguda has ever walked into a kirana and asked "organic unda?". What they ask for is "manchi nei" (good ghee), "bellam" (jaggery), "natu kodi guddu" (country eggs), "ragi java" for the child, and "sugar-free biscuits" for the father-in-law. That is a trust shelf, not an organic shelf.

**What actually sells in a suburb (from having stocked it):** ragi java/malt powder (200–500 g), jaggery (local Medak jaggery in 1 kg, and a jaggery-powder pack), one cold-pressed groundnut oil in 1 L only if it carries the name of a ganuga mill people know, country eggs in a 6-pack from a named farm (sell out by 9am), Dabur honey, Horlicks Diabetes Plus, Sugar Free, NutriChoice/Farmlite biscuits, Saffola oats, Soulfull Ragi Bites at ₹5–10 for kids, Troo Good chikki at ₹10, one A2 ghee only if branded and only in 200 ml, Dabur Chyawanprash from October to February, adult diapers one pack size. That is about 15 SKUs on 6–8 feet, not 27 feet.

**What rots:** 24 Mantra organic dal and rice (people buy 25 kg from the rice merchant), organic masalas, protein bars at ₹70+, makhana at ₹250, millet noodles, unsweetened peanut butter, herbal and green tea beyond one Tata Tea Gold Care, millet dosa mixes at ₹180 (the batter lady is ₹60/kg), Ensure/Protinex beyond one tin. I have thrown all of these away past expiry.

The pre-order weekly basket is a fine idea at 5–8 baskets a week, not 20–40, and only if the FPO van delivers to the store; the day Sateesh is driving 25 km to KPHB on a Saturday for six baskets is the day it stops. Assumption: start the basket in month 3, once the WhatsApp list has 300 households.

### 1.5 Seating: six to ten seats and a customer washroom in 700 sq ft is how you lose the mothers

> "Seating [6–10 seats facing the hot case / none in Store 1]. My pick: 6–10 facing the chai machine and hot case" (§Decisions, 59)
> "Customer washroom: yes/no? Default yes (24 sq ft) because of seating." (note 09, Q7)

**What I'd actually do.** A standing ledge along the window with three stools, charging points, and a bin. No tables, no customer washroom in Store 1. In a suburb, six chairs become the place where four boys sit for two hours with one chai, and the mother with a child who came for milk and idli at 7:30 sees them and does not come in. The Indonesia lesson in the report is exactly this and the report quotes it, then recommends six to ten seats anyway. A customer washroom in a 700 sq ft shop is a cleaning job every hour and a complaint every day; staff-only, with a sign. Assumption: the "rest corner" promise is kept at three stools, drinking water and a charging point; it grows to proper seating in the highway or IT-office format where dwell is the business.

### 1.6 Hours: 6am is late, 11pm is late, 24h is never for Store 1

> "Open 6am–11pm, log sales by hour, and file the G.O.Ms.No.4 exemption only when the 9–11pm slot passes 8% of daily sales" (§12.2)

**What I'd actually do.** Open the shutter at 5:30am. The Vijaya van drops milk at 5:00–5:30; the first customers (walkers, the man going to the BHEL/Patancheru shift, the mother whose child's school van comes at 7) are at the door by 5:45. Close at 10:30pm; the last hour after 10 is one customer every ten minutes and a staff member alone with the cash. Never 24h in a residential suburb: the kirana closes at 10:30 for a reason, the medical shop is the night store, and a night worker alone is a robbery and a staff-welfare case waiting to happen. Do not even keep the "decide at day 90" door open; it distracts. Assumption: the 10:30–11pm hour is under 2% of the day; the 5:30–6am half hour is 3–4% and sets the habit.

### 1.7 Services: not all on day one. The counter person is billing.

> "Services before SKUs ... AEPS, BBPS, recharge, parcel holding and pre-orders should be live on day one, not Phase 2" (§12.7; Decisions 45–47)

**What I'd actually do.** Day one: mobile recharge and bill pay through one aggregator app (two minutes a transaction, no cash float), a ₹15,000 printer for Xerox and WhatsApp print (the most-asked service in any suburb, every school day), and parcel holding (free, no work, a shelf behind the counter). Month 2: AEPS, once the second staff member is settled, because AEPS needs a ₹20–30,000 cash float, a biometric device, a queue of people who buy nothing, and a person who is not also billing. Month 3: courier drop-off, only if DTDC or Delhivery gives a proper franchise code; otherwise you are holding a stranger's parcel with no receipt. Bus tickets and MeeSeva: never in Store 1. Assumption: one services counter with one person doing billing can handle recharge, bills and print; AEPS doubles the counter time per customer and needs its own window.

### 1.8 The 8pm discount announced on WhatsApp trains the whole colony to wait

> "**Fixed window: 8pm, 30–35% off**, tagged at pull-by minus 5 h, announced on WhatsApp to opted-in customers" (§12.4)

**What I'd actually do.** No announced discount window in a neighbourhood store. In Taiwan the discount works because the customer base is a whole city; in a colony of 2,000 households everyone knows by week three that the samosa is ₹8 after 8pm, and the 6pm samosa sale dies. What works: order short (60–70% of yesterday's sell-through for the first fortnight), and clear leftovers in ways that build goodwill rather than a discount habit: "samosa free with chai" to the last ten customers, a plate to the watchmen and auto drivers at the stand at 9:30 (they are your 6am customers), staff dinner, and the rest to a Robin Hood Army volunteer or the nearest ashram with a signed chit. The waste number goes in the POS log as the report says, and the order shrinks the next day. Assumption: planned waste of 5% of hot-food sales in month one, 3% by month three, cleared by giving, not by pricing.

### 1.9 Opening with 1,500 items, a ₹30-lakh-a-month assumption and a State licence

> "Final item count ... My pick: 1,500 at opening, hard cap 2,000." (Decisions 24)
> "a ₹30 lakh-a-month store crosses ₹1.5 cr in months" (Decisions 60)

**What I'd actually do.** Open with 900–1,000 items and an "asked-for" register, and grow to 1,500 over 90 days from what customers ask for. Dead stock is the second most common way a new store dies (after rent), and 1,500 items chosen on paper means 400 of them never sell. Every distributor salesman will "help" you to 2,000 on opening week with his month-end targets. On revenue: plan for ₹10 lakh a month, which is ₹33,000 a day; that is 300 customers at ₹110. It is a good year-one store. Take the State FSSAI licence anyway because it costs ₹2,000 and the Food Safety Officer treats it differently, but do not let the ₹30-lakh assumption flow into stock, staff or rent decisions. Assumption: ₹10 lakh a month in year one, ₹15 lakh by year two if hot food and services work.

### 1.10 Sale-or-return "with a written credit %" on day one, and "buy outright" on hot food

> "**Sale-or-return with a written credit %** for bread, buns, batter and snacks; buy outright for hot-case items" (§12.4)

**What I'd actually do.** Nobody gives a new shop sale-or-return on day one except the bread companies (whose van boy takes expired packs because that is their system). The local bakery will say "cash, and whatever is left is yours" for the first month; after 30 days of steady daily cash payment, ask for puff returns at 50% credit, which they will give because they refry puffs in their own shop. Hot tiffin: never sale-or-return, the report is right; but do not "buy outright" at the vendor's cart price either. The deal is a firm daily order placed by 8pm the previous night, paid by UPI at 8pm the same day, at 30–35% under the vendor's own cart retail; in exchange he gets a fixed volume, daily cash and no haggling. That is worth more to a small vendor than any contract. Assumption: in a new town, written contracts with hot-food vendors are not worth the paper; a one-page spec with a photo of the plate, a daily chit and daily payment is the contract.

### 1.11 ₹300 membership and digital khata on day one

> "Membership [none / ₹300 for 6 months with capped cashback / ₹99 a month]. My pick: ₹300 for 6 months, SuperK-style." (Decisions 53)
> "Digital khata [yes, capped / no]. My pick: yes, capped per customer, WhatsApp reminders." (Decisions 51)

**What I'd actually do.** Nobody pays a new shop ₹300 before they trust it; SuperK's 35–40% uptake is in towns where SuperK is already known. Launch the membership in month 3, when 300 households are on WhatsApp and have bought ten times. Khata: none for the first 30 days, said politely and the same way to everyone ("UPI or cash only, anna, new shop"). From month 2, a khata for named households only (the ones the Agent already knows), capped at ₹500, cleared weekly, and no second cycle until the first is paid. From running a kirana Sateesh knows the real cost of udhaar: not the ₹500, but the customer who stops coming because he owes you ₹500. Assumption: the WhatsApp reminder makes khata workable only if the cap is small enough that nobody is embarrassed.

### 1.12 Delivery on day one with two batched slots

> "Local delivery ... My pick: 2 km, two batches a day, never 10-minute." (Decisions 49)

**What I'd actually do.** No delivery on opening day and no delivery boy on the payroll until there are 20 orders a day. The kirana delivers free because the owner's nephew is sitting idle; a hired boy at ₹13,000 plus a scooter is ₹500 a day, which needs 20 orders at ₹25 of margin each just to pay for him. From week 3, the morning helper takes the scooter for one 4–5pm round within 1.5 km for WhatsApp orders placed by 3pm, water cans included. The Agent collects the orders; the helper delivers. A second slot and a dedicated rider come when the afternoon round is full. Assumption: 20 orders a day by month 3 if the WhatsApp list passes 400 households.

## Part 2. The vendor playbook: hot food and bakery in a new town in four weeks

What follows assumes Sateesh has four weeks before stock-up (weeks -5 to -2 in Part 5), a scooter, a probe thermometer, and mornings free.

### 2.1 Where to look, in order

1. **The 5:30am walk.** For three mornings, walk the 1 km around the site between 5:30 and 7:30. Count the idli carts (bandi), the tea stalls, the tiffin centres with a queue. Note who has a queue of working men (they are cheap and fast) and who has a queue of families (they are clean). Ask the cart man where his batter comes from; follow the batter van if you see one. The batter supplier who serves five carts is your idli-batter vendor, and he usually knows a lady who steams 500 idlis for carts and will do 150 for you.
2. **Industrial-gate mess contractors.** BHEL Ramachandrapuram, the Patancheru industrial estate and the pharma units all run canteens through contractors who cook 300–1,000 breakfasts by 6am. They are 3–6 km away. They already have FSSAI licences, hot boxes and a van. They are the best candidates for upma, pongal, pulihora, curd rice and a mini-meal box, and they are used to being paid on delivered plates with a monthly bill (note 07's SBI tender is exactly this world). Get names from the security guard at the gate; he knows who feeds him.
3. **Tiffin centres with a second counter.** The tiffin centre that already sells parcels at 7am to office-goers can add 100 plates for you, packed. Avoid the one with the longest queue; he does not need you. Pick the clean second-best; you are his growth.
4. **Bakeries with a van.** Not Karachi Bakery (they will not supply a 700 sq ft shop on sale-or-return; buy from their outlet if you want the brand). Find the Patancheru or Miyapur bakery whose van already drops puffs, buns and Osmania biscuits at kiranas and tea stalls; ask the kirana next door which van comes and at what time. Taste the puff at 4pm, not at 8am.
5. **Function-hall caterers.** For festival orders only: boondi laddu, pulihora in bulk, kajjikayalu at Sankranti, bobbatlu at Ugadi. Not daily.
6. **Home cooks and SHG units.** The DWCRA/SHG groups in the villages around Beeramguda make avakaya, gongura, karam podi, sakinalu and chekkalu in season. This is the own-brand pipeline (Part 4, own brand), not the hot case. Ask the municipal MEPMA office or the nearest Mahila Samakhya for the group with a registered unit.
7. **Dairy.** Vijaya and Heritage parlours are cash-and-carry; the Vijaya milk agent for the colony is who you want for milk, curd and majjiga at 5:15am. Register as a retailer with the agent, not a parlour franchisee.
8. **Sweets.** One local sweet shop for gulab jamun cups and laddu; not a brand.

Target list by the end of week 2: two candidate idli/vada suppliers, two candidate rice-item suppliers (one is probably a mess contractor), two bakeries, one dairy agent, one sweet shop, one batter supplier. You will keep one of each.

### 2.2 What to ask, at their place, with the thermometer in your pocket

- What time does the first batch come off? (If after 5:30, they cannot make your 6:15 drop.)
- Who else do you supply? Go and taste there at 9am, when the food is three hours old.
- Do you have an FSSAI number? Most carts do not. Offer to get them Basic Registration (₹100, online, one afternoon) as the condition of the order. The mess contractor will have a State licence; ask for a photocopy.
- What happens on Sunday, on Sankranti, on the day of a family function? Who cooks when you are away? (If the answer is "nobody", you need the backup in 2.6 from day one.)
- If I cut the order by half for a week, is there a problem? If I double it with one day's notice?
- Will you use my boxes? (Yes is the only answer you accept.)
- How do you pack sambar and chutney? (Sealed cups, never loose in a tray.)
- Price for 100 pieces a day, firm, paid daily by UPI at 8pm. Then price for 150.
- Measure the probe temperature of what they hand you. If it is below 60°C at their counter, it will be 45°C at yours.
- Look at the water drum, the cutting area and the hands. Walk away from anything you would not let your mother see.

### 2.3 Prices to aim for (assumptions, Beeramguda, Dec 2026)

| Item | Vendor's own retail | Target landed to Consto | Consto sell | Store margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Idli (each) with chutney/sambar cups per plate | ₹8–10 | ₹5.5–6.5 | 3 for ₹30 | ~33% |
| Medu vada | ₹12–15 | ₹8–9 | ₹15 | ~43% |
| Samosa | ₹12–15 | ₹7–8 | ₹12 | ~37% |
| Veg puff (bakery) | ₹15–20 | ₹9–11 | ₹15–18 | ~38% |
| Pulihora / curd rice 250 g sealed | ₹40–50 | ₹22–25 | ₹35 | ~33% |
| Upma / pongal 200 g sealed | ₹30–40 | ₹16–18 | ₹25–30 | ~38% |
| Boiled egg (store-boiled, electric) | — | ₹6 (egg) | ₹10 / masala ₹15 | 40–55% |
| Osmania biscuit (pack of 4) | ₹32 | ₹20 | ₹30 | ~33% |

Note 07's inferred band (idli ₹6–8) is a touch high for a cart-level supplier at 150 pieces; a mess contractor will be at the top of the band. Opening order: 150 idli, 40 vada, 40 samosa, 30 puffs, 25 rice cups, 40 eggs, which is roughly 100 plates. Expect to throw away 20% in week one and reorder at 70% of sell-through.

### 2.4 How to negotiate sale-or-return without getting laughed at

Do not open with "sale-or-return". Open with "daily cash by 8pm, fixed quantity, 30 days, no haggling". Every small vendor has a dozen shops who pay him at month-end if at all; daily cash is the thing he wants. After he has had 30 days of it, ask for the two things that cost him little: (a) on bakery items, unsold puffs and buns returned at the 3:30pm drop for 50% credit (he refries and resells in his shop); (b) on hot tiffin, a 10% cushion for two weeks ("send 110, I pay for 100 plus whatever sells above"), which becomes firm at the end of the fortnight. Never ask for returns on rice items or sweets; he cannot reuse them and will pad the price. Bread from Britannia/Modern/Bonn comes with the van boy's exchange system already; use it.

Price revision: tell him 5% a year, on the anniversary, nothing in between unless LPG or milk moves 15%. Put that one line on the spec sheet.

### 2.5 The daily hand-over ritual (the thing that keeps the case safe)

**6:15am drop.** Morning staff and Sateesh at the back door. Vendor's boy brings your labelled hot boxes; yesterday's washed boxes go back. Count against the chit: item, quantity, time, probe temperature of one item per box (must read ≥60°C; write it). Staff ticks, boy signs, photo of the chit into the vendor WhatsApp group before 6:20. Shortfall or cold food is written on the chit at that moment, never argued at night. Load the hot case by 6:30, tray tags with load time. Sambar and chutney cups into the chiller.

**3:30pm drop.** Same ritual for the evening load (samosa, puffs, vada, a few rice cups). Morning leftovers are already out of the case by 11:30 (Part 4, Q29).

**8pm settlement.** Sateesh or the shift lead pays the day's chit by UPI, screenshot into the group. Sunday evening: weekly tally against the chits; any mismatch is corrected on the Sunday, never carried.

**Weekly, Thursday:** taste the vendor's food at his counter, not yours. **Monthly:** one unannounced visit to the vendor's kitchen at 4:30am.

### 2.6 Backup when the van does not come

By 6:45 call. By 7:00 the morning person acts without waiting for Sateesh:

1. Batter from the chiller into the electric steamer: idli in 25 minutes (always keep two days of batter; the local batter lady on Mon/Wed/Fri, iD as the branded fallback).
2. Eggs into the electric boiler: 40 boiled eggs in 15 minutes (always keep 60 eggs).
3. Frozen samosa and parotta from the freezer into the microwave and griller (always keep 20 of each).
4. Bakery puffs from the 3:30pm drop that were held back in the chiller.
5. One honest WhatsApp line to the opted-in list: "Idli late today, chai is on us till 8." Name first, under 40 words.

Two misses in a month is the signal to start the "reduce" sequence in 2.7.

### 2.7 How to fire a vendor without losing the morning

Never fire before the replacement has fed the case for a full week. Sequence: (1) new vendor on the afternoon drop only for one week (lower stakes, lets you check consistency); (2) new vendor on 50% of the morning drop for one week, old vendor told "I am trying someone for a product you don't make"; (3) new vendor 100%, old vendor's order cut to zero with the words "I am changing, thank you, here is every rupee I owe you", paid the same day. Never argue about quality in front of his boy, never post about it, and keep buying one thing from him (puffs, sweets) if you can; in a suburb his cousin is your customer and his cart is 200 m from your door.

### 2.8 Bakery specifics

One bakery, on the 3:30pm drop, for puffs, buns, dilpasand and Osmania biscuits; cakes on pre-order only (birthday cakes through the Agent, 24 hours' notice, 50% advance, bakery delivers to the store by 4pm). Bread, rusk and packaged cake come from the company vans (Britannia, Modern, Bonn), not the bakery. Puffs get a 4pm–8pm window in the hot case and a 6-hour clock; anything older is staff tea.

## Part 3. The distributor and brand playbook

### 3.1 Getting distributors to serve one new store

The good news: every distributor in the Patancheru–Miyapur belt already runs a weekly "beat" past the site, because the kiranas are there. The problem is not getting served; it is terms for a new outlet.

- **How to find them.** Ask the two nearest kiranas who their salesman is for HUL, ITC, Britannia, Parle, Coke, Pepsi, Haldiram's, Colgate, P&G, Dabur, Marico, Amul, Vijaya. Photograph a kirana's purchase bill if the owner lets you; the distributor name, GST number and phone are on it. Visit the distributor's godown once in person, meet the owner, not the salesman: the owner decides credit.
- **Opening terms, realistically.** Cash on delivery (or UPI at the van) for the first four to eight weeks; then 7–15 days' credit once two months of bills are clean. Minimum per bill is usually ₹2,000–5,000 and one case per SKU for fast lines; ask for "half case" or "loose" on slow lines (most will, for a new outlet, if you take their fast lines). Say yes to the beat day they offer; being easy to serve is the best negotiation.
- **Schemes to ask for by name.** "New outlet scheme" or "opening display" (free-fill, usually 5–10% extra stock on the first bill); "visi cooler" from Coke and Pepsi (free unit, their drinks only inside it, they service it); Amul's deep freezer (₹25,000 refundable, their ice cream); Parle and Britannia case schemes (5+1); the monthly trade scheme sheet the salesman has in his bag but does not show a new shop; "display" money for the till rail from chocolate and gum companies (small, ₹300–1,000 a month, take it, it does not bend the 1–3 brand rule). Never pay for shelf, never accept a "stand" that locks a wall.
- **Backfill without a beat.** Udaan, Jumbotail and the HUL Shikhar/ITC/P&G apps deliver next day with no minimum beyond a small bill value, cash or UPI, at margins a point or two lower. Use them for week one, for stock-outs between beats, and as the quiet threat that keeps a distributor honest.
- **Expiry and damage.** Photograph transit damage with the bill before the van leaves; claim within 48 hours in the salesman's WhatsApp. Near-expiry: most brands take back through the distributor's "expiry claim" if it is within the brand's window; ask each salesman for his company's rule on day one and write it in the distributor register.
- **The distributor register.** One sheet per distributor: owner, salesman phone, beat day, order cut-off, credit days, minimum bill, scheme this month, claim rule. Pinned behind the counter. When a salesman changes (every six months), the sheet survives.

### 3.3 How to avoid dead stock

- Opening order is half what the salesman writes. Cross out his "target" SKUs; take his top five by his own sales data and your kirana walk.
- No new brand enters without three customer asks in the register or a free-fill. Every new SKU gets a one-line "why" in the POS (the report is right about this).
- Weekly, Sunday night: a "weeks of cover" look at every SKU with more than four weeks of stock; stop reordering, do not discount. Month 2: anything with zero sales in four weeks goes to the end-cap for two weeks, then back to the distributor as a swap (most will swap for a fast line if the pack is clean), then to the ₹5–20 wall as a combo, then written off. Festival and seasonal stock is exempt from the four-week rule until the festival has passed.
- Never buy month-end "schemes" that need three cases to qualify unless it is Parle-G, Maggi, Surf sachets or Coke. The scheme is how the godown's dead stock becomes yours.
- One facing per SKU except the top 20; depth is what rots.
- Expiry walk: the shift lead reads every back label on one aisle a day (six aisles, six days). Anything under two weeks of life on ambient goes to the till rail with a sticker.
